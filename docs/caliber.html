01

In one breath

People with real, world‑class achievements routinely don't know they qualify for an O‑1 "extraordinary ability" visa — and a lawyer's eligibility assessment costs $1,000–$2,000 and takes a week.

Caliber lets you paste your public profile — CV, publications, GitHub, a few press links — and in about a minute returns a grounded verdict: which of the legal criteria you meet, why, where your gaps are and how to close them, and a first‑draft petition letter. Every judgment cites the exact USCIS rule it used. It covers a whole family of achievement‑based visas, tracks live rule changes, and gives F‑1 and H‑1B holders a path forward.

Who it's for

Foreign‑born researchers, engineers, founders and creatives in the US (or hoping to be) — from F‑1 students to O‑1/green‑card candidates who can't afford to find out where they stand.

Why it wins

Real multi‑agent AI doing a lawyer's judgment work, grounded in real law, honest about its limits, in a polished end‑to‑end product with a platform story.

The one‑line pitch

"Caliber reads your achievements the way a US immigration officer would, argues both sides of each legal criterion, and hands you a transparent scorecard plus a draft petition — turning a $2,000, week‑long lawyer assessment into a free, 60‑second one, for a whole family of visas."

02

The problem & who it affects

The O‑1 and EB‑1A are US pathways for people of "extraordinary ability." Unlike the H‑1B, they have no lottery and no annual cap — but they're poorly understood, so thousands of genuinely qualified people never pursue them. Meanwhile F‑1 students and H‑1B workers face a maze of deadlines and odds with no clear map.

Three real frictions

You don't know if you qualify

Eligibility hinges on meeting a set of dense legal criteria most people can't map to their own résumé.

Finding out is expensive

A lawyer's assessment is $1,000–$2,000 and days of back‑and‑forth, just to learn whether it's worth filing.

The advice is opaque

You get a yes/no, rarely the why, and almost never a concrete plan to strengthen a weak case.

Who this hits

International students on the H‑1B cliff, foreign‑trained researchers, immigrant founders — exactly the Open Atlas audience.

The gap in the market: today you get either a free static quiz that just asks you to self‑rate, or an expensive opaque service. Nothing does the actual reasoning, transparently, for free — or connects the visas into one journey.

07

The core principle: AI proposes, code disposes

This is the design decision that makes Caliber accurate enough to trust — and proves it's a system, not an API key with a UI.

The rule: the LLM extracts evidence and argues each criterion. Deterministic code holds the legal rules, does all counting and math, and renders the final verdict. Retrieval guarantees every claim points to real law. The AI never decides the outcome — it informs a rule that does.

Deterministic — code / rules / data

  • The verdict itself — counting "≥N of M met" is a rule, not an opinion.
  • Criteria & thresholds — hardcoded per visa from the regulations.
  • Citation grounding — the AI can only cite policy passages that exist in the store.
  • H‑1B odds & salary percentile — real math on real published data.
  • Counts — citations, stars, publications fetched from APIs, never estimated.
  • STEM‑OPT check — lookup against the DHS designated‑degree list.

AI‑handled — the right job for an LLM

  • Extraction — messy PDF/HTML → structured evidence.
  • Mapping — which criterion a given achievement supports.
  • Per‑criterion reasoning — the Advocate / Examiner arguments.
  • Gap advice — contextual, field‑specific runway.
  • Drafting — Met criteria + evidence → petition prose.
  • Policy summaries — dense rules → plain language + "affects you."

Why this answers "aren't you just wrapping an API?" The decision layer is ours. The model is a bounded extractor/reasoner inside guardrails it can't override.

08

The multi‑agent architecture

US visa adjudication is inherently adversarial: a lawyer files the strongest case, an officer looks for reasons to deny (a "Request for Evidence"). Caliber mirrors that with a per‑criterion panel — so the structure reflects reality, not novelty for its own sake.

The panel — run per criterion

AgentRoleReal‑world analog
AdvocateArgues the evidence meets this criterion, citing the strongest factsThe petitioner's lawyer
ExaminerArgues why USCIS would reject it — surfaces the likely RFEThe adjudicating officer
AdjudicatorWeighs both against retrieved policy → Met / Partial / Gap + confidence + reasoningThe decision

Then code takes over: it counts the Met criteria and applies the N‑of‑M rule. The panel judges each criterion; it never decides the overall verdict.

Efficiency (deliberate): the full 3‑agent panel only spins up on borderline criteria — a slam‑dunk Met or an obvious Gap resolves in a single pass. Cheaper, faster, and it's exactly the contested criteria where the two‑sided argument is worth showing on camera.

Standout feature: the Examiner's objections are an RFE predictor — "here's how an officer would push back, before you file." That's the 20‑second clip that carries the demo video.

10

Policy Watch — living rule tracking

A feed of recent US immigration changes, AI‑summarized and classified by visa — including F‑1 and H‑1B, which affect the widest audience. This is what turns Caliber from a one‑time quiz into a product you return to.

Each update card shows

what changed · who is affected · visa tags · effective date · plain‑language summary · link to the source rule.

Where the AI does real work

Summarize

Dense Federal Register legalese → one clear sentence.

Classify

Tag each change to visa types + an impact level.

Personalize

Cross‑reference a change against the user's saved assessment: "affects criterion 3 on your scorecard."

Deterministic vs AI: code fetches the Federal Register API, date‑filters, dedups, and pulls effective dates from structured fields; the AI summarizes, classifies, and reasons about personal impact.

Honest caveat (stated in the demo): major rule changes don't happen every month for every visa. The feed shows real Federal Register items — volume varies, and we never fabricate a change. F‑1 and H‑1B churn most, so they anchor the feed.

13

End‑to‑end system flow

How an assessment moves through the system, from click to PDF:

1 Auth — user signs in with Google (Auth.js). Session tied to their account.

2 Select — user picks a visa; the engine loads that visa's rule pack.

3 Ingest — links + résumé submitted. Server fetches GitHub + OpenAlex, parses the PDF.

4 Extract — Extractor agent turns everything into a structured evidence list (typed JSON).

5 Retrieve — each criterion paired with its governing policy text (fixed map) + relevant examples (semantic search).

6 Deliberate — per‑criterion panel returns Met/Partial/Gap + reasoning + citations. Full panel only on borderline criteria.

7 Decide — code counts Met criteria, applies the N‑of‑M rule, computes any numeric checks. This is the verdict.

8 Present — scorecard renders: verdict, per‑criterion cards, gap runway.

9 Act & export — Drafter agent writes the petition letter on request; user downloads the full PDF.

Data trust boundary: user‑supplied links and files are treated as untrusted input — fetched server‑side, parsed, and never used to drive actions beyond this assessment.

Risks & how they're handled

Accuracy / hallucinated law

Grounding + deterministic verdict; the model can't invent criteria or cite non‑existent policy.

Latency (many agent calls)

Panel only runs full on borderline criteria; parallelize criteria; Gemini Flash is fast.

Data source down

Every critical source is user‑provided or a stable free API; graceful fallback to résumé‑only.

Scope creep

Strict must/should/stretch split; nothing on the critical path depends on a stretch item.
